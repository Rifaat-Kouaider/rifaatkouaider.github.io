The Senate Wants MFA Mandatory in Every Practice. Start Your 12-Month Clock Now.

October 14, 2026 · 3 min read

Listen
0:00

On September 30, the U.S. Senate did something it almost never does. It agreed unanimously on a healthcare bill.

The Health Care Cybersecurity and Resiliency Act passed by unanimous consent. It still needs the House before it becomes law. But when 100 senators agree on something, you don’t wait for the House to start preparing.

Here’s what the bill does. It orders HHS to rewrite the HIPAA Security Rule around three non-negotiables: multifactor authentication, encryption of patient data, and regular penetration testing. Not guidance. Requirements.

Now the part most coverage skips, and it’s the part that matters to you. The bill gives penalty relief to practices that can prove they’ve been running recognized security practices for at least 12 months before an incident. Twelve months. The clock starts when you start, not when the bill becomes law. Every month you wait is a month you can’t get back.

The honest read: nothing on this list is exotic. MFA, encryption, a pen test. This is the table stakes list, the stuff your IT vendor should have set up years ago. Congress isn’t asking for anything fancy. It’s asking for the basics, with enforcement behind it.

So here’s your head start. Five things, in order:

  1. Turn on MFA everywhere. Email, EHR, VPN, remote access. No exceptions, including the physicians. This is the single cheapest breach prevention you can buy, and it’s usually free.
  2. Verify your PHI is actually encrypted. Most modern EHRs encrypt by default. “Most” and “default” are doing a lot of work in that sentence. Check.
  3. Get a penetration test. Or at minimum, a real vulnerability scan from someone who isn’t selling you the firewall. Then fix what it finds, in order of severity, not convenience.
  4. Document everything. The safe harbor doesn’t reward good intentions. It rewards 12 months of provable practice. Write it down, date it, keep the reports.
  5. Audit who has access. Terminated employees, old vendor accounts, the biller who left in March. If this sounds familiar, read last week’s post.

The bill might stall in the House. It might get watered down. Doesn’t matter. The practices that treat this as a deadline will be fine either way, and the ones that wait for Congress to force their hand will be the case studies.

No sales pitch. Just start the clock.
